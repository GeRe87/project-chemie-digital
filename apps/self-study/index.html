1. Course Overview

Course Overview
Chemometrics and Applied Statistics connects statistical reasoning, quantitative data analysis and modeling with problems from analytical chemistry. The course emphasizes understanding what a method measures, the assumptions behind it, how results are interpreted, and how calculations can be reproduced.

2. About the Lecturer

About the Lecturer
Gerrit Renner — Analytical Data Science, Instrumental Analytical Chemistry, University of Duisburg-Essen
Teaching and research connect chemometrics, analytical data science and instrumental analytical chemistry, with a focus on processing and interpreting complex analytical data.

2. random variable

random variable
A real-valued random variable is a measurable function from the outcomes in a sample space to real numbers. Its probability distribution describes the probabilities associated with the values or sets of values it can take.
A random variable is not one observed result. A die result or a measurement value is a realization of a random variable under the stated probabilistic model.

3. random variable

random variable
A relation such as X = θ + ε is an additive measurement model, not a definition of a random variable. It is appropriate only when a stable quantity θ and an additive random error term ε are justified; measurement noise must not be identified with measurement uncertainty.

4. discrete random variable

discrete random variable
A discrete random variable takes values in a finite or countably infinite set; its distribution can be represented by probabilities assigned to those values.
For a fixed sampled volume and counting protocol, the observed number of colonies is integer-valued and can be modeled as a discrete random variable.

5. continuous random variable

continuous random variable
A continuous random variable has a continuous distribution. In the common absolutely continuous case, probabilities are assigned to intervals by integrating a probability density, and any single exact value has probability zero.
A measured concentration may be modeled as a continuous random variable over the physically admissible range when the measurement model treats concentration as continuously valued.

1. arithmetic mean

arithmetic mean
\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_iMathematische Formel für arithmetic mean
  • The arithmetic mean can be computed for numerical data when addition and division by the number of observations are meaningful; normality is not required.
  • Strong skewness or extreme observations can reduce how representative the arithmetic mean is as a center.

2. expected value

expected value
  • Expected value is the probability-weighted average defined by an integrable random variable's probability distribution.
  • It is a property of the distribution, not the arithmetic mean of one observed sample.
\operatorname{E}[X] = \sum_{x \in \mathcal{S}} x\,P(X=x)Mathematische Formel für expected value
  • The sample arithmetic mean is a statistic computed from observed data.
  • Under an appropriate sampling model it can estimate a population expected value, but it is not semantically identical to that expected value.

3. law of large numbers

law of large numbers
  • For independent identically distributed observations with a finite expected absolute value, the sample arithmetic mean converges to the expected value as sample size grows.
  • The law of large numbers concerns convergence of sample averages.
  • Standard-error formulas and approximate normal sampling distributions are separate results, not definitions of the law of large numbers.

4. geometric mean

geometric mean
  • For strictly positive observations, the geometric mean is the nth root of their product.
  • Equivalently, it is the exponential of the arithmetic mean of their logarithms.
G = \left(\prod_{i=1}^{n} x_i\right)^{1/n}, \qquad x_i > 0Mathematische Formel für geometric mean
G = \exp\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ln x_i\right), \qquad x_i > 0Mathematische Formel für geometric mean
  • The geometric mean is appropriate for strictly positive quantities when equal weighting on a logarithmic scale is scientifically meaningful.
  • It suits multiplicative factors or ratios but is not a universal replacement for the arithmetic mean for every rate.
Two equal successive periods have multiplicative factors 2 and 8. Their geometric mean is 4, and applying factor 4 in each period preserves the total factor: 4 × 4 = 2 × 8 = 16.

6. harmonic mean

harmonic mean
A traveler covers two equal-distance sections at 10 m/s and 2 m/s. Total distance divided by total travel time gives 10/3 m/s ≈ 3.33 m/s, equal to the harmonic mean of the two speeds.
  • For nonzero observations with a nonzero reciprocal sum, the harmonic mean is n divided by the sum of their reciprocals.
  • Common rate applications use positive values.
H = \frac{n}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} 1/x_i}Mathematische Formel für harmonic mean
  • For positive rates over equal distances or equal quantities in the rate denominator, the harmonic mean gives the overall average rate.
  • For equal time intervals, the arithmetic mean of the rates applies instead.

7. median

median
For 2.1, 2.3, 2.0, 2.2 and 45.0 NTU, the arithmetic mean is 10.72 NTU and the median is 2.2 NTU. The example illustrates the greater resistance of the median to one extreme observation; which summary is scientifically preferable still depends on the question being asked.
  • A median is a central value with at least half of the observations no greater and at least half no smaller.
  • For an ordered finite sample, use the middle order statistic for odd n and the arithmetic mean of the two middle order statistics for even n.
\tilde{x}=\begin{cases}x_{((n+1)/2)}, & n\text{ odd}\\\frac{x_{(n/2)}+x_{(n/2+1)}}{2}, & n\text{ even}\end{cases}Mathematische Formel für median
  • The sample median depends on order and is substantially less sensitive to extreme values than the arithmetic mean.
  • It is robust to outliers, but extreme observations are not guaranteed to have no effect in every dataset.
