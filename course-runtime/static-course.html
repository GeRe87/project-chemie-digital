Variables

Variable

In statistics, a variable is a characteristic or quantity that can take different values within a stated dataset, study, model or experiment. Its values may be observed, measured, assigned or controlled.
Introductory Statistics

Why do we need variables?

  • Describe experiments through individual aspects of a system.
  • Examine aspects separately, while stating the conditions.
  • Describe relationships between these aspects.

For a discrete random variable with finite expected absolute value, weight each possible value by its model probability, not by observed sample frequency.
\operatorname{E}[X] = \sum_{x \in \mathcal{S}} x\,P(X=x)

Example: Does arithmetic preserve growth?

ARITHMETIC FACTOR
Which constant factor reproduces 1600 cells from 100 in two equal periods? Apply it twice: 100 × m × m = 100m². Factor 5 predicts 2500 ≠ observed 1600. The arithmetic mean fails this growth question.
\begin{aligned}A&=(2+8)/2=5\\100\times5^2&=2500\ne100\times2\times8=1600\end{aligned}
A
Arithmetic average of growth factors 2 and 8: A = 5.
100, 2
Initial count 100; exponent 2 applies one factor over two equal periods.

Residuals Share One Exact Constraint

\sum_{i=1}^n d_i=0
Why deviations about the arithmetic mean sum to zero
d_i=x_i-\bar{x}

Sum both sides over the observations

\begin{gathered}a_i=b_i\ (i=1,\ldots,n)\\\Longrightarrow\quad\sum_{i=1}^n a_i=\sum_{i=1}^n b_i\end{gathered}\sum_{i=1}^n d_i=\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar x)
Sum indexed equalities

Add the left sides and the right sides of equalities that hold at every index. This gives a consequence; the summed equality alone does not recover every individual equality.

\sum_{i=1}^n d_i=\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar x)

For each observation, dᵢ = xᵢ − x̄. Add these equalities for i = 1 through n. The result sums the left-hand residuals and the right-hand deviations. This implication needs no probability assumption. It does not say that one summed equation can recover all individual observations.

Precision, Trueness and Bias

Precision
Scenario: repeat the same control measurement. **Precision** concerns agreement among repeated results. State the measurement conditions and independent units. Small spread does ==not establish trueness==.
Trueness
Scenario: compare an average with a suitable reference. **Trueness** concerns agreement of the long-run average with the reference. A small observed SD does not answer that question. Finite data only estimate the long-run behavior.
Bias
Scenario: a tightly grouped series is centered above the reference. **Bias** is the difference between the expected result and the reference. An observed mean difference estimates bias with uncertainty. A series can be precise and systematically shifted.

Three injections from one vial

Standard Error Decreases as 1/√n

\begin{gathered}\operatorname{SE}_n=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\quad\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0\\\operatorname{SE}_{4n}=\frac12\operatorname{SE}_n\end{gathered}

  • **Square-root law:** at fixed population spread, four times as many independent observations halve the SE. Individual measurement spread does not shrink.
  • **Scope:** independent observations with common finite variance. Correlation or systematic bias can leave ==uncertainty that averaging does not remove==.

What Have We Learned?

  • A common mean can hide different observed spreads.
  • MSD describes data; sample variance estimates population variance under assumptions.
  • Fitting the mean constrains residuals, not the original sampling design.
  • SD has the original unit; RSD expresses spread relative to the mean magnitude.
  • Precision and analytical decisions require stated measurement conditions.
